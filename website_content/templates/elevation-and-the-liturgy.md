I will go unto the altar of the Lord. 
These are the words that for close to 1000 years were said by priests and ministers as they ascend to the altar before they begin the office of mass, and it sets the precedent for what is about to be done. In the Roman liturgy we are not simply encompassed by heaven and the saints, we are elevated to them. This is shown so frequently in the mass, the priest climbs the altar step, we are  called to behold and look to the heavens and God, we look up to Christ as we receive Him, and he is elevated for all, even the priest, to crane their necks toward Him. 

<div class="academic-heading">
The Real Elevation to Heaven
</div>

When we talk about the mass and the words and actions contained within, we often refer to it as symbolic, but we don't seem to understand what that word means anymore, just because it
symbolic, does not mean that is merely a symbol, the mass is the actual events of salvation history that we are taking a part of every time we pray it, not symbolically, but actually. But what does this mean in the Roman liturgy especially?

In the Roman Liturgy, every word and prayer is about bringing us up to heaven, up until the consecration when Christ comes down, but elevation is just a direction, it is a feeling, and the mass excels more so at this when it is at it's fullest. The mass begins when the priest reaches the altar and the entrance antiphon is begun, the first part of the office of the mass, but the elevation begins earlier; during the procession an organ prelude is often played, and this tends to set the town for the whole mass. Most frequently played with horns or  joyful flutes, it sets the tone of something majestic, and immediately brings us out of this world and sets everything aside. The music of the mass sets this tone throughout the entire liturgy. This is most evident in the late 19th and early 20th century when the French organ tradition reached it's peak. Masses would frequently never have a moment of silence, and would be filled with music that painted the mass with a variety of tones and texture that are foreign for most small parishes these days.


<div class="academic-heading">
Kyrie
</div>

In Vierne's "Messe Solennelle" you hear a play between a choir and two organs. Within the first movement of the mass "Kyrie" you immediately notice that the organs are not just accompanying the choirs, but they are accompanying the choirs, painting the words with intense meaning "Lord have mercy, Christ have mercy, Lord have mercy" with the immediate feeling of melancholy and dread, and a final time, as though begging, with a strong resolution of hope.
