Something that always intrigues me about medieval Catholicism is this idea of finding the peace of Christ. In today's modern liturgies it is often explained that the peace of Christ is the greeting we give to one another in Christ, however this is only one side of the story. To understand what the medievals thought we need to look at both the action, historical context, what that means, and how we can apply that today.

<div class="academic-heading">
The Evolution in the Roman Liturgy
</div>

The peace that is given in the liturgy of the current ages is from the priest to the people. However, this was not the case until the late 20th century. The medieval form of the sign of peace came from a kiss between the priest, deacon, and sub-deacon. One of our first thoughts when we hear or witness this is "Is this not the same sign that Judas gave the Romans?" and although this is the case, a kiss in European cultures would be a greeting reserved to close friends, signifying the relation between the trinity of ministers as one being personal through Christ. But why do we have the sign of peace to begin with? When Christ was resurrected although he bore the wounds of his crucifixion, he was not recognized by the disciples, and the way he addressed them is what told them who he was "Peace be with you." Now we can look at the liturgy and look at to who the priest is specifically. Something that well catechized Catholics will be intimately familiar with is that the priest acts in Persona Christi when he offers the sacrifice, and when we can see these actions from the priest the ministers re-emphasize who he is in the person of at the moment, and offer reassurance.

When we exchange the sign of peace now, it is frequently just an informal greeting of one another during the liturgy, exchanging the hospitality of Christ, but we also should not forget that we are to be receiving the reassurance of Christ in the mass and treat this moment with the reverence do, and keep Him and that reassurance He gives first-most in our hearts.

<div class="academic-heading">
The peace of Christ, finding Him in Silence
</div>

This brings us to the meat of this article, what is the peace of Christ? A medieval idea is finding the peace of Christ in prayer, and this is an extension of the reassurance that was given to the disciples. What is the peace we are talking about though? It is a peace of complete surrender and acceptance. This is going to sound weird for people who have not experienced it, but it is truly feeling God's presence, either through Christ in the Eucharist, or His Saints. 

The feeling is something that is hard to describe, but I will try my best. It is a complete comfort in the moment, focused entirely on Christ, one where you could joyfully sit in that presence for all of eternity and be happy, it is like a high that you constantly want to chase again after you experience it once. It can stop prayer altogether, allow you to focus on what you are praying, or just put you a state of complete reception. It is like being held in your mothers arm when you are scared at night feeling nothing but love, and then wanting to share that with others, and live in that for your whole life. We need to be careful though because the consolations are not an end, but the means of deeper relations.

It is weird to think about this, because it is something scary to think about(for me at least) that there is a side to my faith that isn't just actions and words, thoughts and deeds, but also feelings and relationship; it is something that medievals would have been intimately familiar with though, knowing and trying to build relations with everyone in their community is a necessity of Catholicism, and back when your whole tone of 200 people all went to church together at least once a year, it was something that was integral to proper society. I do not think it is a coincidence that we see mystics and ascetics start showing up more frequently in the Western world beginning in the middle ages, it was when societies were stabilizing and proper communities were  being built, when monasticism and religious life really begin becoming the norm for more of the populus. People were obsessed with giving their life to God, and even laity wanted to experience this fervor, and one of these things that emerged was the idea of complete peace with Christ.

<div class="academic-heading">
Peace in the Modern Age
</div>

As I am writing this in Fall of 2025, we seem to be building relations with objects other than people. The language used by companies to correlate the relation between the product and you is that of intimacy, not that of a tool, but a friend, and while some may say that the comfort and relations we have with our phones and the internet are not even real, I think we can safely say that they are, chemically and emotionally. Phones do bring people the relation and comfort that close friends would bring to one another. The one thing they never bring though that real relations can, is peace. 

Making true friendships devoid of technology and that are christocentric is difficult, even in churches without devout Catholics. The idea of of relations being an extension of Christ's peace is something that is not even thought about by most not matter who good their relations are, and that makes budding these relations difficult. Something that has helped me find people I can truly talk to and fully love, is searching for my own holiness and relation with God, and extending that relation to others, and building a similar one with them, and being around those who are doing the same and doing it earnestly; Being around people that are chasing the same holiness is life changing, and it begins a domino effect for those around you.

I encourage everyone to find that peace through prayer, focusing on the object of the prayer, the form or matter. For further readings I would recommend reading [St. Francis de Sale](https://archive.org/details/ASelectionFromTheSpiritualLetters/page/n13/mode/2up) and [Ignation Prayer](https://www.jesuitseastois.org/ignatian-prayer). Something that helped in my spiritual journey was something an Orthodox priest told me, all prayer needs to go the heart, and not just stay in the head or it can become an object of sin instead of relation.
